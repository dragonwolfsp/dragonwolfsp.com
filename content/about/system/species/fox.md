## Overview

Every fox has exactly one tail. That never varies, and it stays the practical way to tell a fox from a [kitsune](../kitsune/).

Foxes are canids, but they sit well apart from wolves in how they live — solitary or loosely familial rather than pack-forming, omnivorous rather than committed carnivores, and built for pouncing rather than pursuit.

## Physical traits

Slender-bodied with long legs, a narrow muzzle and large triangular ears tuned to low-frequency sound, which is how they locate prey under snow or soil. The pupils are vertically slit rather than round — unusual among canids, shared with cats, and associated with ambush hunting.

The tail, the "brush", is disproportionately full: used for balance in the turn, for signalling, and wrapped around the body for warmth when sleeping. It is often white-tipped.

The mousing pounce is diagnostic — a high vertical leap ending nose-first.

## Magic

The species fixes nothing. Some foxes are tied to magic heavily and some barely at all, and what form it takes is entirely their own.

## Lineage

Foxes and kitsune can interbreed, which is less surprising than it looks given the resemblance. Kitsune and [vulperin](../vulperin/) can as well.

Foxes and vulperin cannot.

## Shared instincts

Foxes share real instincts: they are opportunistic, cautious and inventive, they cache surplus food and remember where, and play persists well into adulthood in a way it does not for most canids. Feeling tends to be legible across the whole body at once, and adaptability is genuine — the animal thrives nearly everywhere by staying curious.

Those are instincts rather than temperament. A fox is free to differ from any of them, and many do.

## Where the name comes from

The red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) is the most widely distributed wild carnivore on the planet, occupying the northern hemisphere across nearly every habitat and thriving in cities. Foxes are canids but sit apart from wolves and dogs in behaviour: solitary or loosely familial rather than pack-forming, omnivorous rather than committed carnivores, built for pouncing rather than pursuit. They maintain a vocal repertoire of around forty distinct sounds, including the scream commonly mistaken for something in distress.

The slyness the European tradition assigns them is not something we carry. That reading says more about foxes raiding farmsteads than about foxes, and the folklore that produced Reynard is not folklore we draw on.
