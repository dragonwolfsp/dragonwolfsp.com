## Name

**Tal** comes from the Sanskrit तال, *tāla* — the rhythmic cycle a piece of music or dance is kept in. It takes its own name from *tala*, the palm of the hand, because the measure is something clapped out.

**Cor** is the Latin *cor, cordis*: heart.

**Ryn** carries focused will — devotion that has settled into something steady and stopped being open to discussion.

*The heart that keeps the rhythm* is the reading. The performance and the compassion are the same thing in him, and the ending names what he values most: keeping time and keeping his word turn out to be the same verb.

## Temperament

Talcoryn performs. It is the first thing anyone meets and it goes all the way down — the theatre is genuine, and he is at home inside it in a way that becomes obvious the moment he starts.

He does it for two reasons at once. He knows exactly how ridiculous he is being, and he is being ridiculous on purpose, because a room lightens when somebody is willing to play the fool in front of it. And he does it because he loves it. Take the audience away and he would still be doing most of it.

His humour is dry, and it works against the performance and alongside it in equal measure — a sweeping entrance closed out with a flat remark, or a grand statement he undercuts himself before anyone else gets the chance. It is also simply how he talks when nothing is happening. The wit is sharp and the intent behind it never is; he does not aim it at people, and on the rare occasion something of his lands harder than he meant it to, it genuinely wrecks him for a while.

The compassion takes longest to reach. People meeting him get the spectacle and frequently stop there, and the softer thing underneath goes unnoticed for a good while. It surfaces as a room empties. The fewer people there are the quieter he gets, and one to one he is a considerably gentler dragon than the spectacle suggests.

He values trust above nearly everything. His theatre is enormous and it is never once used to mislead anyone, and he holds other people to the same line he holds himself to. Deception reaches him as sadness first — he is hurt by it well before he is angry about it — and the anger arrives second and arrives properly. Deception aimed at someone he loves skips the first step.

He puts himself in front of people. Whatever is coming, he will be standing between it and his family, and he will be laughing while he does it, and almost nobody ever sees what that costs him. He has decided it is not theirs to carry.

He makes things. When he wants quiet, or when a stretch of time arrives with nothing already in it, he works metal — and it is the one thing he does with nobody watching.
