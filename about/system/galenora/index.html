Galenora

A leviathan who steps into the way early, and rarely needs anything more than that.

Gender
Female
Pronouns
she/her
Species
Leviathan

Name

Galen comes from the Greek galēnē — the calm of the sea, the stillness that settles over open water. It names a condition of the water itself, which is the right sort of calm for her: something the whole body of it is doing.

Ora comes from the Latin ōra — shore, edge, border. The line where the water stops.

The calm that holds the edge is the plain reading. She is the stillness and she is the boundary, and neither half of her works without the other.

Temperament

Galenora’s quiet is a decision, and she keeps making it. She talks when there is something worth saying, easily and at length if the conversation earns it, and none of the silence around her comes from reluctance or nerves. She prefers a room with less noise in it, and she has arranged her own share of the noise accordingly.

Her warmth arrives through contact. She sits with people and she holds them — an arm, a coil of tail, a shoulder to lean on for as long as leaning is wanted — and a great deal of what she has to offer is delivered that way, with the words coming second. The conversation that happens while she is holding somebody tends to be quiet, unhurried, and further in than the one they would have had standing up.

Protection, for her, is a step. When something is coming for someone she loves she puts herself in front of it, early, and says nothing about it afterwards. There is no display in any of it — no swelling up, no warning anyone off, no explanation of what will happen if the thing in front of her keeps coming.

The power is real and she reaches for it last. Her water control is very strong, and her kinetic magic is precise enough to take a body apart at the level of its cells or put it back together again. She uses it to heal. Standing in the way tends to settle the question long before any of the rest becomes relevant, which appears to be exactly how she likes it.

People who have known her a long time describe the same two things: that her company is restful, and that the restfulness is only half of what is going on. She is warm the entire time. She is also the reason nothing has reached them.

Physical description

Galenora is warm before she has said anything, and immovable in the same breath. She occupies a considerable amount of room and has no interest in filling it — the space is simply taken, the way a headland takes a stretch of coast, and people arrange themselves around her without being asked to. Her attention covers everything within reach of her and rests heavily on nothing. People lean on her without deciding to, and generally without noticing they have.

In her anthro form she stands around nine feet, drawn broad through the shoulders and chest, with heavy muscle lying smooth beneath the scales. The torso runs long, keeping the serpent’s proportions where a humanoid frame would have cut them short. Her weight sits low. Anyone who has tried to move her once she has decided otherwise comes away having learned that the decision and the physics are the same problem.

She travels her range constantly and thinks nothing of it. In water she goes serpentine, twelve feet of her at full length. In company she settles into the anthro middle. Indoors, in close quarters, and whenever she wants to hold someone comfortably, she comes up to the near-human end and stands about seven and a half feet. Every point of it is unmistakably her — the same deep blue, the same golden-brown eyes, the same banding — and watching her move along it reads as somebody changing posture.

Her scales are deep blue with a silvery sheen worked through them, broad and plate-like across the back and flanks, growing finer at the throat and belly. They are set extraordinarily close. Run a hand along her and the gaps register under the fingertips; look at the same stretch of her and there is nothing to see, and anything trying to find its way between them would need luck more than aim. Along the spine and the outsides of her limbs the plating thickens, which is precisely where a blow would land. Wet, the whole of her catches light and throws it back. Dry, she goes matte.

Banding rings her length, evenly spaced and clearest when she is serpentine, and it carries through every form she holds. Her underbelly runs a brighter blue than the rest of her, and in her anthro and near-human forms that same brightness sits across her chest.

Her face is long and draconic through the muzzle, clean-lined the whole way, carrying neither ridging nor horns. It settles into calm on its own — the resting expression of somebody comfortable and listening. The closer she comes to human, the softer the structure grows, the muzzle shortening and the lines gentling until what remains is a face that has kept its scales and given up its length.

Her eyes are a rich golden brown, warm against all that blue, with pupils built wide for deep water and gathering considerably more light than most rooms have to offer. A nictitating membrane slides across them underwater, and it does the same when she is thinking — a slow translucent blink that holds half a second too long and tells anyone watching that she has gone somewhere with what they said.

Her ears are fin-like, folded close on land and fanning wide the moment she is under. A dorsal ridge follows the line of her spine. At the shoulders sit trailing membranes, soft-textured and unhurried, that drift and turn in water and settle against her when she is dry.

Her paws are broad, heavy and fully webbed, oversized in the otter’s way, with thick pads beneath. They shrink as she moves toward the human end of her range, and they never once shrink to a human size — her hand is the larger thing at every point on the spectrum. The claws are long, and in her anthro and near-human forms they retract completely. The webbing pulls taut and translucent when she spreads her digits and folds away invisibly when she does not.

The tail is thick through the base and tapers slowly along its length, carrying a fin down the underside. It is fully prehensile, and she uses it the way most people use an arm — around a waist, across a lap, wrapped once about whoever she is holding. Toward the human end of her range the join between body and tail sharpens into something distinct. In her serpentine form there is no join at all, only one continuous line and no telling where she stops being torso.

She moves smoothly and continuously, and considerably more quietly than nine feet of anything has a right to. On land she is unhurried and entirely capable. In water she is effortless and very fast. Speed arrives whole when it is wanted, at full value from the first movement, with nothing leading up to it. The interposition works the same way: one step, taken early, putting her between a person and whatever is coming, finished before most people have registered that it began.

Water answers her when she feels something strongly. It leans her way — a surface tilting a fraction toward her, a filled glass finding a new level, a current reorganising itself around wherever she has decided to stand. Her kinetic work shows as economy. She never moves more than the task takes, and the difference between her and anyone else doing the same thing is how little of it there was. The precision runs fine enough to work at the level of individual cells, and she uses it to heal. What else it could do, she keeps to herself.

What she feels shows in two places. The tail moves, and where it has put itself — and what it has put itself between — is the most reliable thing about her. And she reaches: a hand at a shoulder, an arm drawn round, the tail settling across a lap, because contact is the first thing she does with any feeling that arrives. She is quiet and she is not closed, and the people who know her have never had the slightest trouble reading her.

Being near Galenora is like deep, still water, with everything that implies about the amount of it underneath. It is also like being behind something. People stop bracing in her company without noticing they have stopped, and frequently without having noticed they were braced at all.

She smells of fresh clean sand with a faint salt undertone beneath it — an open shore, sun on dry grains, the air a little way back from the tideline.

Galenora is deep, warm water with soft light coming down through it. She is the quiet that was chosen, the arm that goes round somebody before they have thought to ask, and the body that arrives between them and whatever was coming for them. She has never once had to raise her voice to do any of it.
